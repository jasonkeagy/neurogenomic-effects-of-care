<strong>Summary</strong>

Offspring are often influenced by the care they receive from their parents. However, we know little about the underlying neurogenomic mechanisms. Using a half-sib design, threespine stickleback fish were either raised by their fathers or hand-reared (“orphaned”) for ten days before being raised in a common-garden environment. As offspring developed, they were tested in one of three behavioural assays: an open field assay with a simulated predator attack, a social behaviour assay with a simulated predator attack, and a scototaxis assay. Offspring that received paternal care were bolder and more social. Fish in the open field assay had their brains sampled one hour following the simulated attack; brains were sampled at the same time from full-sib controls. These brains were processed for gene expression (via 3’Tag-seq) and chromatin accessibility (via ATAC-seq). Paternal care treatment affected brain gene expression, but sex was also a major factor, despite the fish being reproductively immature. The predator attack in the open field assay affected expression of fewer genes. Sex, and to lesser extent, paternal care treatment, also influenced chromatin accessibility at a whole genome scale. Our findings further our understanding of the mechanistic basis for response to variation in early-life experiences generated by parents.
